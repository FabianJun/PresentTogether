# Method cards for explaining and presenting

Use these cards when you suggest a change to a talk. Name the method, say why it works, show how to apply it to *this* talk and say when it does not fit. The person should be able to use the method again without you.

Each card notes how strong the evidence is. **Strong** means repeated experiments or meta-analyses. **Moderate** means good studies with limits. **Practice** means widely used advice without much direct testing. **Standard** means a published technical standard, not a research finding. Do not overstate a card's evidence when you explain it.

Explain a method fully the first time and briefly after that. Never list every card at once; pick the three to five that matter most for this talk.

## Message and story

### One core message

- **What it is:** the single sentence the audience should remember, written before the slides.
- **Why it works:** a clear goal tells you what to cut, and gives the audience a thread to follow. *Practice.*
- **How:** ask the person: "If the audience remembers one sentence, what is it?" Check every slide against it. Put it near the start and again at the end.
- **Typical mistake:** a topic instead of a message. "Ocean acidification" is a topic; "The ocean is absorbing our CO₂, and shell-building animals pay the price" is a message.
- **When it does not fit:** exploratory sessions where the point is an open question. Then the open question is the thread.

### And, But, Therefore

- **What it is:** a three-step story shape. *And* sets the scene, *But* introduces a problem or surprise, *Therefore* gives what follows. Proposed by Olson (2015).
- **Why it works:** it turns a list of facts into a question the audience wants answered. Narrative texts are understood and remembered better than expository texts (Mar et al., 2021). *Moderate for narrative in general; practice for this template.*
- **How:** before: "Interest rates. Compound interest. Examples." After: "We all save a little *and* expect it to grow slowly, *but* the growth is not linear, *therefore* when you start matters more than most people expect."
- **Typical mistake:** "and, and, and": a chain of facts with no *But*, so nothing is at stake.
- **When it does not fit:** a procedure people simply need to follow step by step. A clear sequence works better there.

### Situation, complication, resolution

- **What it is:** a close relative of And, But, Therefore, common in business and policy talks: describe the situation, name the complication that makes it a problem, then give your answer.
- **Why it works:** the audience hears the problem before the answer, so the answer has a reason to exist. *Practice.*
- **How:** before: "Our project: background, methods, results." After: "Our club's members save regularly (situation). Many start late and lose years of growth (complication). We propose a small standing order from the first pay cheque (resolution)."
- **Typical mistake:** a complication so mild that nobody needs the resolution.
- **When it does not fit:** open, exploratory topics without one answer; use an open question instead.

### A real case or a person

- **What it is:** carry an abstract idea through one concrete case or person the audience can follow from start to end.
- **Why it works:** a specific case gives the abstract idea something to hold on to, and a person gives the audience someone to care about. *Practice.*
- **How:** before: "Oysters are affected by acidification." After: one oyster hatchery from a published report, followed through the seasons when its larvae died, with the measurements behind each step. Use a sourced case; never invent one.
- **Typical mistake:** an invented case presented as real, or a case that is more vivid than representative. Say clearly what is real and what is illustrative.
- **When it does not fit:** when the case would expose real people or sensitive details. Use a clearly fictional composite instead.

### Open with a question, not an agenda

- **What it is:** start with a puzzle, a surprising fact or a prediction the audience makes, instead of a table of contents.
- **Why it works:** an open question creates a reason to listen; making a guess first helps later learning (see *Pretesting and prediction* below). *Moderate.*
- **How:** turn the core message into a question and ask it in the first minute.
- **Typical mistake:** a rhetorical question nobody actually thinks about. Give people a few seconds, or ask them to write a guess down.
- **When it does not fit:** formal settings where an agenda is expected. Give the question first, then a brief agenda.

### A signpost after the hook

- **What it is:** right after the opening, one or two sentences on where the talk is going: "First we see why…, then what…, and at the end you decide…".
- **Why it works:** people follow a talk more easily when they know its shape; unlike a reader, a listener cannot flip back. *Practice.*
- **How:** keep it to three parts at most, said aloud; a slide is optional.
- **Typical mistake:** a ten-point agenda slide read out in full before anything interesting has happened.
- **When it does not fit:** a talk built on a surprise that the signpost would give away. Signpost the question, not the answer.

### End where you started

- **What it is:** the ending answers the opening question, returns to the opening case or repeats the core message in a new light.
- **Why it works:** it closes the loop the opening created and repeats the core message at the moment people are most likely to remember it. *Practice.*
- **How:** before: "Conclusion: it is a serious problem. More research is needed. Questions?" After: "Remember your guess for €1,000 after 30 years at 5%? Here is the real number: about €4,300."
- **Typical mistake:** new material on the last slide, or an ending that only summarises the agenda.
- **When it does not fit:** rarely. Even an open-ended talk can end by restating its open question more sharply.

### Replace "Any questions?"

- **What it is:** close with a specific question or task instead of the open "Any questions?"
- **Why it works:** a concrete prompt is easier to answer than an open invitation, so more people join in. *Practice.*
- **How:** "Which of the three options would you choose, and why?" or "Talk to your neighbour for one minute: what still surprises you?" Then take questions, and end on your core message rather than on the last question.
- **Typical mistake:** waiting in silence, then closing with "OK, thank you".
- **When it does not fit:** formal Q&A formats where the chair runs questions; you can still frame the first question.

## Slides and visuals

### Reduce cognitive load

- **What it is:** working memory can hold only a little at once. Anything that does not help understanding competes with what does (Sweller, 1988; Sweller et al., 2019).
- **Why it works:** removing unnecessary material leaves capacity for the hard idea. *Strong.*
- **How:** one idea per slide; cut decorative images, background detail and text the speaker will say anyway. Split a dense slide into two or three.
- **Typical mistake:** keeping everything "for completeness". Move the details to a handout or a backup slide.
- **When it does not fit:** reference handouts meant to be read later can be denser than projected slides.

### Multimedia principles

Mayer (2020) reviews evidence-based principles from many experiments. *Strong for most of these in controlled studies.* The most useful for talks:

- **Coherence:** leave out interesting but irrelevant material.
- **Signalling:** highlight what matters, for example with an arrow, colour or a headline.
- **Redundancy:** do not project the same full text you are saying aloud; show a picture or key words instead.
- **Spatial contiguity:** put labels on the diagram, not in a separate legend.
- **Segmenting:** reveal a complex process step by step.
- **Pre-training:** introduce key terms before the explanation that uses them.

*Typical mistake:* a full paragraph on the slide, read aloud word for word. *When they do not fit:* effects are smaller for audiences who already know the topic well (see *Fade support as people gain expertise*).

### Assertion–evidence slides

- **What it is:** each slide's headline is a full-sentence claim; the body is visual evidence (a chart, photo or diagram), not bullet points.
- **Why it works:** in a study with engineering students, assertion–evidence slides led to better comprehension and lower reported load than default bullet slides (Garner & Alley, 2013). *Moderate.*
- **How:** before: headline "Results", five bullets. After: headline "At 5% a year, starting ten years earlier leaves over 60% more", one chart.
- **Typical mistake:** a full-sentence headline above the same five bullets.
- **When it does not fit:** title, agenda and transition slides; audiences who will read the slides alone without a speaker.

### Choose the chart for the message

- **What it is:** pick the type of chart from what you want to show, not from what the software suggests.
- **Why it works:** the right form makes the comparison visible at a glance, so the audience does not have to work it out. *Practice.*
- **How:** change over time → line; comparing amounts → bars, sorted; parts of a whole → stacked bar, or a pie with very few parts; relationship between two measures → scatter; a process → a simple flow of boxes and arrows. Highlight the one line or bar that carries the message and grey out the rest.
- **Typical mistake:** a table of 14 rows when the message is one difference. Show the difference; put the table in the handout.
- **When it does not fit:** an audience that needs exact values to look things up. Then a clean table is right.

### One consistent style

- **What it is:** the same fonts, colours and positions on every slide, with colour used for meaning, not decoration.
- **Why it works:** consistency lets the audience stop noticing the design and notice the content; a change in style then signals something important. *Practice.*
- **How:** two fonts at most; one accent colour for "this matters"; the same place for headlines; the same colour for the same thing on every slide.
- **Typical mistake:** a new colour scheme per section, or four colour scales on one map.
- **When it does not fit:** rarely. Deliberate breaks are fine when they mark a real change, such as the activity.

### Readable contrast and size

- **What it is:** text that people at the back and people with low vision can read.
- **Why it works:** WCAG 2.2 level AA asks for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text (W3C, 2024). *Standard.*
- **How:** dark text on a light background or the reverse; check colours with a contrast checker; never rely on colour alone to carry meaning. Check font size by looking at the slides from the back of the room, or at a small preview on a laptop.
- **Typical mistake:** pale grey text on white, or red and green as the only difference.
- **When it does not fit:** always applies.

## Explaining a hard idea

### Concreteness fading

- **What it is:** start with a concrete example, move to a simplified picture, then to the abstract rule or formula.
- **Why it works:** concrete examples give meaning; abstract forms let people transfer the idea (Fyfe et al., 2014). *Moderate; promising but not universal.*
- **How:** €1,000 in a savings account → a growing bar chart → the formula.
- **Typical mistake:** staying with the example and never showing the general rule, so people cannot apply it to a new case.
- **When it does not fit:** audiences who already know the concrete case well; start nearer the abstract form.

### An analogy, and where it breaks

- **What it is:** explain something unfamiliar through something familiar, then say clearly where the comparison stops working.
- **Why it works:** the familiar case gives people a structure to map the new idea onto; naming the limit stops them from carrying over the wrong parts. *Practice.*
- **How:** "The ocean is like a sponge for CO₂: it soaks up a lot. But unlike a sponge, the water itself changes chemistry as it does."
- **Typical mistake:** an analogy that is more memorable than the idea it explains, with no limit named.
- **When it does not fit:** when the audience does not know the familiar case well, or when the differences matter more than the similarities.

### Pretesting and prediction

- **What it is:** ask people to answer or predict *before* you explain. Predict–Observe–Explain is a classroom form: predict, see the result, explain the difference (White & Gunstone, 1992).
- **Why it works:** trying to answer before learning improves later recall, even when the first answer is wrong (Richland et al., 2009). *Strong for pretesting; POE is a method, not separately tested here.*
- **How:** "What will €1,000 be worth in 30 years at 5%? Write down a number." Reveal the answer. If most guesses are too low, explain why.
- **Typical mistake:** revealing the answer before people have committed to a guess.
- **When it does not fit:** when a wrong guess could embarrass people. Keep answers anonymous or in pairs.

### Pauses and short summaries

- **What it is:** a short pause after a key point, and a one-sentence summary at the end of each main part.
- **Why it works:** listeners need a moment to process before the next idea arrives; a summary helps people who drifted off to rejoin. *Practice.*
- **How:** after the hardest slide, stop for three seconds. Before moving on: "So far: …. Next: …."
- **Typical mistake:** filling every gap with "um" or the next slide.
- **When it does not fit:** very short talks, where one summary at the end is enough.

## Attention and taking part

### Active learning

- **What it is:** the audience does something with the content: answer, discuss, sort, decide.
- **Why it works:** a meta-analysis of 225 studies in university science and maths found higher exam scores with active learning, and students in lecture-only courses were about 1.5 times as likely to fail (Freeman et al., 2014). *Strong for university STEM; evidence outside STEM is thinner.*
- **How:** plan one short activity per main idea, not one for its own sake.
- **Typical mistake:** an activity that is fun but does not require thinking about the core idea.
- **When it does not fit:** very short talks where one well-placed question is enough.

### Where to place the activity

- **What it is:** decide what job the activity does in the story: an opening hook, the turning point or consolidation near the end.
- **Why it works:** an activity tied to the story's structure moves the talk forward instead of interrupting it. *Practice.*
- **How:** a hook raises the question (a prediction); a turning point makes the audience feel the problem (a decision with a surprising result); consolidation lets them use what they learned (a sorting task or a short case).
- **Typical mistake:** the activity placed at the very end, when time is short and attention is lowest, with no link back to the talk.
- **When it does not fit:** formats where activities are fixed by the session; then link to it explicitly in your notes.

### Retrieval practice

- **What it is:** people recall what they have learned, rather than hear it again.
- **Why it works:** recall tests produced better long-term retention than restudying (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006); practice testing is one of the most useful learning techniques (Dunlosky et al., 2013). *Strong.*
- **How:** at the end, ask "Write down the two things you remember most" before your summary slide, not after.
- **Typical mistake:** showing the summary first, so people only recognise instead of recall.
- **When it does not fit:** right after first exposure to something completely new; give a moment of explanation first.

### Peer instruction

- **What it is:** pose a conceptual question, people vote, discuss with a neighbour, then vote again (Crouch & Mazur, 2001).
- **Why it works:** explaining to a peer exposes gaps; in one long-running physics course it produced larger conceptual gains than lecturing. *Moderate.*
- **How:** choose a question where about half the room is likely to be wrong.
- **Typical mistake:** a question so easy that everyone agrees, so there is nothing to discuss.
- **When it does not fit:** factual questions with nothing to discuss.

### Everyone can take part

- **What it is:** design the activity so every person has a real, safe way to join: quiet people, people on their own, people without a phone and people using assistive technology.
- **Why it works:** if only the confident few answer, the rest of the room becomes an audience again. *Practice.*
- **How:** anonymous answers or pairs before open discussion; a paper or show-of-hands alternative to phones; time to think before anyone speaks; no one forced to share personal matters.
- **Typical mistake:** "Who wants to answer?", which always reaches the same three people.
- **When it does not fit:** always applies, though the means depend on the room.

### Plan for attention

- **What it is:** treat attention as something the talk earns again and again, not a fixed span.
- **Why it works:** the popular "attention drops after 10 minutes" claim rests on weak evidence; attention seems to depend more on the presenter and the task than on a timer (Wilson & Korn, 2007; Bradbury, 2016). *Moderate.*
- **How:** change the mode when the content changes: a question, a demonstration, a short activity, a story. Mark long stretches of one-way listening on the attention curve.
- **Typical mistake:** treating a fixed timer as a rule. Do not insert breaks at fixed intervals just because of a rule of thumb.
- **When it does not fit:** short talks with a single, well-paced explanation.

## Coaching the person

### Fade support as people gain expertise

- **What it is:** guidance that helps beginners can get in the way of people who already know a topic (Kalyuga et al., 2003).
- **Why it works:** experts must process help they do not need. *Strong.*
- **How:** adapt coaching per area with the [competence profile](competence-profile.md), fading support as the person shows the method. For the audience, give more scaffolding to novices and more open tasks to experts.
- **Typical mistake:** explaining the same method in full every time it comes up.
- **When it does not fit:** a person who asks for more detail; follow their lead.

## Myths to avoid

Correct these gently if they come up, and do not use them yourself.

| Myth | What the evidence says |
|---|---|
| "People can only pay attention for 10 minutes." | The claim rests mostly on secondary citations and weak data (Wilson & Korn, 2007; Bradbury, 2016). |
| "Our attention span is now 8 seconds, shorter than a goldfish." | The figure could not be traced to a real study (BBC News, Maybin, 2017); attention depends on the task. |
| "Teach to each person's learning style." | There is no adequate evidence that matching teaching to learning styles improves learning (Pashler et al., 2008). |
| "Communication is 7% words, 38% voice, 55% body language." | Mehrabian's figures came from experiments about feelings and attitudes; he says they do not apply otherwise (Mehrabian, n.d.). |
| "People remember 10% of what they read and 90% of what they teach." | The "learning pyramid" percentages have no sound source (Letrud & Hernes, 2018; Masters, 2013). |
