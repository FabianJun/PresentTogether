# Singularity - Speaking Notes

Game setup or configured deployment: https://github.com/scaled-thinking/present-together/blob/main/docs/SINGULARITY-EXAMPLE.md#run-locally

The moderator alone controls the shared slides and game. Open "4 speakers" in that console and give each speaker their private notes link. Their page shows only their own slides and cannot change the projection. Each speaker can download their own PDF.

SAY is spoken text. DO contains short presenter instructions. NEXT is a suggested handover. Keep the source links for questions.

Aim for 10-12 minutes of presentation, plus 10 minutes of activity. The 11-minute schedule includes explanations and short pauses. There are no opening or closing polls. Game notes follow each round; discuss the final result before handing over to Speaker 4. The QR code first appears, enlarged, at the game lobby. Rehearse once.

Activity: explain the matrix (2 minutes), play four rounds (6 minutes), discuss the result (2 minutes).

Speaker 1: introduction, definitions and human control (slides 1-4). Speaker 2: scenario endings, acceleration, case study and ethics (5-8). Speaker 3: game theory, all four simulation rounds and debrief (9-10). Speaker 4: solutions and conclusion (11-12). Each speaker speaks in one uninterrupted block. The shortened narrative texts allow roughly 11-12 minutes at 100-110 words per minute with transitions and brief pauses. Speaker 3 leads the activity, including the round prompts and audience decisions. A single moderator operates the controls; speakers ask them for the actions in DO.

PowerPoint has 17 static slides with editable notes. Use the browser for live votes and the game. The last two slides are worked examples.

## 05 / Two endings in AI 2027.

SLIDE 05 - Two endings in AI 2027.
SPEAKER 2 | planned slot 1:10

SAY
AI 2027 has two endings.

In the race ending, leaders keep using a system they suspect is misleading them. To stay ahead, they give it more access. Dependence grows until they cannot reliably stop it.

The authors imagine a catastrophe. This is speculative. Possible risks include cyberattacks, biological weapons and loss of control.

In the slowdown ending, outside researchers help find a safer path. But power remains with a small group. Who gets a voice?

Our game explores cooperation too. Checks take time, can expose secrets and leave you vulnerable if the other side cheats.

## 09 / Your prisoner’s dilemma.

SLIDE 09 - Your prisoner’s dilemma.
SPEAKER 3 | planned slot 2:00

SAY
We will play a short simulation to understand the ethical dilemma in the AI race towards a possible singularity.

You advise OpenBrain. DeepCent decides at the same time. Neither sees the other's choice.

If DeepCent cooperates, Race gives you five points. Cooperation gives you three.

If DeepCent races, Race gives you one point. Cooperation gives you zero.

Race pays more either way. Yet both racing gives one each, while both cooperating gives three each. That is the prisoner's dilemma.

Points measure competitive advantage, not people's safety. Workers, patients and people whose data is used carry risks too.

Would you trust a promise when cheating pays?

DO
Use the same original matrix throughout. Point to 5 versus 3, then 1 versus 0. Explain why Race pays more for the current round either way, while mutual cooperation gives both more than mutual racing. Explain that Cooperate keeps oversight; Race puts speed first. Open the game lobby after the question about trust.

NEXT
Now join your side. We will make four decisions.

SOURCES

## 11 / Make cooperation safer than racing.

SLIDE 11 - Make cooperation safer than racing.
SPEAKER 4 | planned slot 1:50

SAY
We support cooperation, independent checks and consequences for breaking shared rules.

Our matrix rewards racing. Oversight protects people but leaves the pressure to stay ahead.

Both sides need the same safety duties. Inspections help check promises, but take time and reveal sensitive work. Who checks the inspectors?

Breaking those rules must have credible consequences. Otherwise each side still has a reason to race.

Technical safeguards matter too: test AI, limit its access to tools and data, and monitor its use. People need the power to intervene.

Misuse means people cause harm with AI. Misalignment means AI pursues unintended goals. Tests can still miss failures.

Workers, patients and people outside both sides need a voice. Energy use also affects communities and the environment.

DO
Connect the unchanged prisoner's dilemma to the need for shared rules, inspections and credible consequences in the real world. These are proposed solutions, not extra game choices or a second payoff matrix. Include technical safeguards, human oversight and affected people.

NEXT
That is difficult when rivals want to win.

## 12 / Who controls your future?

SLIDE 12 - Who controls your future?
SPEAKER 4 | planned slot 1:50

SAY
Asked about AI losing control and launching cyberattacks, Trump replied: "We're leading China in AI, and whoever wins AI, wins."

Our game illustrates how competitive pressure can weaken safeguards.

How can we maintain control over increasingly complex AI systems?

First, test AI, limit its autonomy and access, and monitor it.

Second, keep people able to question decisions, intervene and stop harmful use.

Third, support shared rules, inspections and penalties so rivals have a reason to cooperate.

AI 2027 helps us explore competition. Keeping control also needs technical work and people who take responsibility.

Your job, your data and the services you use are affected. What protection would you want?

DO
Read the quote and connect it to the pressure to race. Answer the opening question through technical safeguards, human oversight and shared rules. Explain AI 2027 as the competition example within this wider problem. Finish with the audience question.

NEXT
Thank you. Our sources follow.
